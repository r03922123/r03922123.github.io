Position bias: why click logs lie and how to correct them

October 31, 2026 · 6 min read · ranking · recommendation · evaluation

Every ranking system that learns from its own clicks has the same blind spot. People click what they see, and they see what is on top. Train a model on raw clicks and it learns that the top slot is good, not which items are good. This post sets out the problem as a simple model of clicking, shows with a small simulation how badly raw click-through rate misleads, then walks through the standard corrections and when each one fits.

The feedback loop

A ranker shows a list. Users click mostly near the top. Those clicks become the next training set, in which the items that were on top look best, so they stay on top. An excellent item that first appeared at rank 9 collects few clicks, looks mediocre, and stays at rank 9. The system confirms its own past choices and cannot find better ones.

The bias is not only "people are lazy". In an eye-tracking study of web search, Joachims et al. [1] swapped the top two results without telling users. People still clicked the first slot more often, even when it now held the less relevant result. Position carries trust as well as visibility.

A model of a click

The standard way to separate the two effects is the examination hypothesis [2]: a user clicks an item only if they examined it and found it relevant, and whether they examine it depends only on its rank \(k\):

\[ P(c = 1 \mid d, k) \;=\; \underbrace{P(e = 1 \mid k)}_{\text{propensity } p_k} \cdot \underbrace{P(r = 1 \mid d)}_{\text{relevance } \rho_d} \]

Raw click-through rate estimates the product \(p_k \rho_d\), not \(\rho_d\). Two items with the same relevance at ranks 1 and 8 can differ in CTR by whatever factor separates \(p_1\) from \(p_8\).

Inverse propensity weighting

If we knew \(p_k\), the fix would be one line: divide each click by the probability that its position was examined [3]. The weighted click is an unbiased estimate of relevance:

\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{c}{p_k}\right] = \frac{p_k\,\rho_d}{p_k} = \rho_d . \]

For learning to rank the same weight goes on each clicked item's loss term, so a model trained on logged clicks optimizes, in expectation, the loss it would have had with true relevance labels:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{IPS}} = \sum_{(d,\,k)\,:\,c_d = 1} \frac{\ell(d)}{p_k} . \]

A small simulation makes the size of the problem concrete. Ten items with known relevance are shown in a fixed order, examination falls off as \(p_k = 1/k\), and we compare raw CTR with the IPS estimate:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_items, n_sessions, eta = 10, 200_000, 1.0
relevance = rng.uniform(0.05, 0.6, n_items)           # P(click | examined), unknown to us
propensity = (1 / np.arange(1, n_items + 1)) ** eta   # P(examined | rank k)

ranking = rng.permutation(n_items)                    # ranking[k] = item shown at rank k
rank_of = np.argsort(ranking)                         # rank_of[i] = rank of item i

examined = rng.random((n_sessions, n_items)) < propensity          # columns are ranks
clicked = examined & (rng.random((n_sessions, n_items)) < relevance[ranking])

naive = clicked.mean(0)[rank_of]                      # raw CTR per item
ips = (clicked / propensity).mean(0)[rank_of]         # each click weighted by 1 / p_k

Estimating the propensities

Everything above assumes \(p_k\) is known. It has to be measured, and there are three common routes.

  • Randomize on a small slice of traffic. Shuffle the top results, or swap the item at rank \(k\) with the one at rank 1, for a small fraction of requests. Because relevance is now independent of rank, the click rate at each rank is proportional to \(p_k\) [3][4]. The cost is a worse experience for the users who see shuffled lists.
  • Fit a parametric curve. A power law \(p_k = (1/k)^{\eta}\) is a common choice [3]; the exponent depends on the surface. A search results page, a feed and a horizontal carousel decay very differently.
  • Estimate without intervention. Treat examination and relevance as latent variables and fit both from regular logs with expectation–maximization [5], using the fact that the same query–item pair appears at different ranks over time.

Variance and clipping

IPS is unbiased but can be very noisy. A click at rank 50 with \(p_{50} = 0.02\) counts fifty times as much as a click at rank 1, so a handful of deep clicks can dominate a training batch. The usual remedy is to cap the weight at some \(\tau\):

\[ w_k = \min\!\left(\frac{1}{p_k},\; \tau\right). \]

Clipping deliberately reintroduces some bias, since deep items are now under-credited, in exchange for a large cut in variance. It is the same bias–variance trade-off as capping importance weights in counterfactual evaluation [6], and \(\tau\) is best chosen on held-out randomized traffic, not by convention.

Learning propensities and relevance together

Randomization costs user experience, and a hand-fit curve may be wrong. The dual learning algorithm [7] avoids both by noticing a symmetry in the examination hypothesis: if you knew relevance, you could estimate propensity by inverse relevance weighting, just as IPS estimates relevance by inverse propensity weighting. DLA trains a ranking model and a propensity model jointly, each using the other's current estimates as its correction, and both improve together. The cost is a harder optimization problem with two coupled models to debug.

Position as a feature

A simpler and very common approach is to give the model the position it was shown at during training, and to fix that input to one constant at serving time, so every candidate is scored as if it sat in the same slot. Done naively, the position input can get tangled up with other features. Two published designs keep it separate by construction:

  • PAL [8] factors the predicted click probability into \(P(\text{seen} \mid k) \cdot P(\text{click} \mid \text{seen}, d)\), with a separate module for each. At serving time only the second module is used.
  • A shallow tower [9]: position and other bias features feed a small separate network whose output is added to the main model's logit during training and dropped at serving time.

Both are forms of the examination hypothesis built into the architecture. Neither removes the need to check that the "bias" side really learns only bias: if position is correlated with item quality in the logs, which it is by design, some quality signal can leak into the position side.

Comparing rankers: interleaving

Position bias also distorts evaluation. To compare ranker A with ranker B, team-draft interleaving [10] merges their lists into one: the two rankers take turns picking their highest remaining item, with a coin flip deciding who picks first each round. A click credits the ranker that contributed the item. Across many requests both rankers fill every position equally often, so position bias cancels out by design rather than being corrected statistically.

Because every user sees both rankers at once, interleaving reaches a confident verdict with far less traffic than splitting users into two arms: in large-scale comparisons, Chapelle et al. [11] found it one to two orders of magnitude more sensitive. Its limit is that it tells you which ranker is better, not by how much on business metrics, and it produces no training signal.

Which correction to use

MethodUse it forWatch out for
IPSTraining on logged clicks when propensities are knownHigh variance from deep clicks
Clipped IPSThe same, with bounded varianceBias toward the top; choose \(\tau\) on data
RandomizationMeasuring propensities directlyCosts user experience on the randomized slice
EM / DLAPropensities without a randomization budgetHarder to optimize and debug
Position as a featureA quick, architecture-level correctionQuality signal leaking into the position input
InterleavingDeciding which of two rankers is betterNo effect size, no training signal

In practice these combine: a little randomized traffic to estimate and audit propensities, a position-aware model or IPS-weighted loss for training, and interleaving to decide which model ships.
